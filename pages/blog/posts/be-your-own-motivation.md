To whom it may concern, dear diary… today I have zero motivation, and nothing gets done. Choices feel heavy, attention collapses, and procrastination looks like laziness.

If you recognize this, first let me tell you that you are, in all probability, not lazy. In Germany, we have a saying: *"Es gibt kein schlechtes Wetter, nur schlechte Kleidung"* (There is no such thing as bad weather, only bad clothing). Just as with this proverb, motivation is often misunderstood. Most people treat motivation like solar power — they sit around waiting for the "mood" to strike before they can move. Let's not do that – that's neither as practical nor as dependable as building your own motivation manufacturing system to rely on. I'll outline a structured approach to building a reliable internal motivation system.

Before we start …

## What is Motivation?

Motivation comes from the Latin *movere* — to move. It's a liminal thing: both a momentum (the spark) and the movement itself. In practice, motivation behaves like a loop:

Movement → Momentum → Movement → Momentum…

Being "in the mood", having momentum, is a lovely state, but it's unreliable as a starter motor. Too often, we wait for the mood to come and then expect movement to follow. The trick is to treat movement as the starter: small actions create momentum, and momentum shifts mood. So, we manufacture the first movement.

Why are we lacking momentum/motivation?

- Too little clarity,
- Too little energy,
- Feeling emotionally overwhelmed,
- Just don't know how or where to start,
- Strong perfectionism,
- Don't feel qualified enough,
- Fear of failure,
- …

There are many different reasons why we procrastinate, and sometimes it's a blend of those reasons. It's important to recognize that we are all different. My approach centers on three main steps: filtering out unnecessary drains on your energy and attention, generating the momentum needed to get started, and facilitating by creating an environment that makes productive action easier and unproductive action harder. In the sections ahead, I'll walk you through each pillar and offer a practical toolkit you can choose from to build your own motivation system. Such a system comes in handy to rely on if a normal flow of things just doesn't want to get going.

So, here is how to build one.

## Filter: Reducing Cognitive Load

This is about protecting your inner battery. There are two strategies to do so. One is filtering out stuff in advance. The other is to reduce the decisions you have to make on an already full plate.

The filtering out starts with saying no. Which is hard for some of us, I get it. But just like on an airplane in emergency mode, you have to take care of yourself first before you can take care of others.

First, you; then, please help others.

![Airplane oxygen mask safety instructions: put on your own mask before helping others](/images/blog/be-your-own-motivation/safety_instructions.webp)

Saying "no" isn't about being mean; it's about self-protection so you can better help others if you have battery.

So in your daily life, give the "Hell Yes … or NO" rule a chance. If it's not a "Hell Yes," it's a "No." Don't go to that party if it's not a "Hell yes"; say no if a colleague asks for help they can get elsewhere.

If it's a "Must Do" and not a "Hell Yes," make it easier on yourself by building a calendar culture so you don't have to remember events and tasks you said yes to. Add reminders to not get inundated by the existing stuff on your agenda.

If your plate is already full, reduce the number of decisions you have to make. Think of decision-making like a battery. Every choice — what to wear, what to eat, which email to open first — takes a small sip. I remember times in my life when I would stop at a supermarket after work, stand in front of the shower gel rack, and have no energy whatsoever to decide which one to buy. It's usually not a big decision, but it was probably the 100th of that day.

Physical clutter also creates a source of constant "micro-decisions" that act as a subliminal cognitive drag. A cluttered desk or room pokes your mind to be distracted. Clear spaces don't magically create willpower, but they remove tiny drains on your attention.

Pick one surface and clear everything you don't need for the next 24 hours. Put items in a single box labeled "later." That clear desk reduces micro-decisions all day. Also, that is an action that accomplishes something, which rewards you with a tiny shot of dopamine.

Can you remember that it was always easier to eat an apple as a kid when it was cut into pieces? Prepare your environment and the task as bite-sized as possible.

## Generate: Make Yourself Move

Start anywhere; it's not about perfection.

The 2-Minute Rule: Lower the barrier to entry until it's basically impossible to fail. Don't "Write a Report" — just "Open a Word Doc and write one sentence". Once you're moving, the "appetite comes with the eating." That's about generating momentum.

DONE is better than perfect.

And then there is the fake-it-till-you-make-it … *identity edition*. One of my favorite tricks for my morning run is putting on my workout clothes before my brain is even fully awake. By putting on those clothes, I am priming the identity of an athlete. Even if I don't feel like training yet, the friction of *not* training has increased because I'm already dressed for it. I'm basically rehearsing the role of the person I want to be in 10 minutes. The now-me wants to procrastinate, but the future-me 10 minutes from now is a runner.

And while we're at "faking" stuff. You can work with artificial deadlines, so you precommit in order to limit your fuzzy choices in the future. If you could finish a ticket next week or next month, set yourself a deadline even if it doesn't have one, or set your personal deadline to submit that talk earlier than the call for talks ends. However, treat that one with caution so as not to get further overwhelmed.

As procrastination can be due to situational overwhelm, try to activate your parasympathetic nervous system by breathing 4-7-8 — count to 4 while breathing in, hold your breath for 7, then exhale for 8. Do this 4–7 times.

Also, some physiological sighs in between will lower your anxiety and heart rate measurably. Take a full, deep breath through your nose, take a quick, short second inhale on top to fully pop open the air sacs in your lungs, and then let out the breath through your mouth (like a deep sigh of relief). Three times.

While deadlines, artificial or real, are like a push-to signal for me, a reward is a pull-towards signal, which I prefer. I like rewards, so I regularly make delayed-gratification deals with myself. It's a simple contract: "If I complete task X, I get reward Y, and I enjoy this reward." I trained my brain to link the effort of a *for-me* difficult task with a *for-me* valuable reward. So much so that I get happy by challenging myself with *for-me* hard things. This hasn't always been the case, but it was a training thing. I've basically taught myself to "embrace the suck".

## Facilitate: Engineering your Environment

Prepping your environment. Instead of the self-flagellating "What's wrong with me?", ask "What's wrong with my situation?". Prep your environment to facilitate the way of least resistance. It goes both ways — a positive and a negative prepping of your surroundings.

Positive prepping is like greasing the machine, making it easier to reach the good stuff.

Going to the gym? Have your stuff ready BEFORE you have to head out. Right next to your door, all packed up, ready to go.

Difficult problem for the next day? Have your IDE open to the exact line you need to start from and/or have your next prompt ready to shoot in the morning.

Negative prepping is like creating speed bumps for yourself, making it harder to reach the bad stuff. Remove temptations before you need to draw on your battery to resist them.

- Decided to watch less TV? Put the remote in a drawer, make it hard to reach.
- Still not both hands on the keyboard? Put your phone in another room for 90-minute intervals.

If you can't put your phone out of hand's reach, how about setting it to black and white to reduce distraction?

## Our Brains on Dopamine

Dopamine is more than the colloquial "reward for finishing" or a simple "pleasure hormone". It's a neurotransmitter of anticipation, prediction, pursuit, progress, and motivation — basically, the "do-it-again" signal.

As with a metabolic glucose post-peak low, there are "cheap" dopamine spikes (scrolling, sugar) that give us a quick dopamine hit but lower our dopamine baseline temporarily afterward, leaving us less motivated for other tasks as the brain compensates by downregulating receptors, leading to a temporary deficit state. Also, it narrows our focus so that eventually *only* that stimulus can trigger a spike. So we need to teach our brain to enjoy small, healthy forward movement (small spikes), not just the finish line (large spikes). The habit we want to train is the dopamine-action loop:

Action → Small progress → Small Dopamine → More action

Small, consistent wins (micro-actions, checklists, visible progress) keep the loop running. Don't wait for a giant win to trigger your internal reward. Make small progress visible. Create a simple progress list, adding one line for every (tiny) win. It's for your eyes and brain only, so don't worry about how big the wins need to be to make it to the list!

As a side-hack: Dopamine sensitivity is restored by good sleep. So when we're sleep-deprived, we tend to not only get crankier and unfocused but also crave immediate rewards more and find it harder to delay gratification, so getting solid sleep is a simple anti-procrastination move and should be as non-negotiable as possible.

## Recap: A System for the Long Game

We've applied practical techniques for conserving our *cognitive resources battery* by filtering out obligations in advance and reducing decisions, applying strategies to generate the activation energy for starting tasks, and facilitating by systematically removing/creating barriers so that taking action becomes the path of least resistance.

We've dipped into how our brain's dopamine dynamics work, why sleep matters for reducing procrastination by restoring the brain's ability to value future rewards, and why taking action is the most reliable way to build motivation for more action.

Being your own motivation doesn't mean pushing harder. It means building a system where moving becomes the natural thing to do. Now we have a ready-to-use toolkit to stop sabotaging our future self and start building motivation that works even on tough days.

Having said all that: don't forget to be kind to your future self. Leave your (working) environment a better place for your future self in the morning, a place where it's easier to start.

Also, Kaizen. This movement-momentum-motivation journey is not a sprint, but a marathon — and that's ok.

![Kaizen (改善): 1% better every day](/images/blog/be-your-own-motivation/kaizen.png)

So overall, overcoming procrastination is challenging but achievable; it's hard, but not insurmountable. Bit by bit. And you will have better days and worse days. Tonight: Prep one thing for your Future Self. Clear your desk, put your phone away, or lay out your gym clothes. Give your future self the gift of momentum.
